～Literature References in Taylor Swift Lyrics～

As one of the most influential singer-songwriters of our time, Taylor Swift’s music captures various emotions and life events, such as the “almost” romance that never really begins, the heartbreak of losing a beloved family member, and self-doubt. One of the most fascinating features of her songs is the abundance of classical literary references in the lyrics. As a foreign languages and literature major and a “Swiftie” (a fan of Swift), I find this technique of weaving classical literature into her lyrics to be what keeps her songs captivating me. Behind those melodies, which classics has she revitalized?

The first one is one of her most popular songs: "Love Story.” The theme of the song revolves around Shakespeare's "Romeo and Juliet", describing a love affair that the families oppose. However, Swift gave the couple a different ending: Romeo successfully persuaded Juliet’s father and finally proposed to his beloved. One of the lines, "That you were Romeo, you were throwing pebbles," echoes the original script, in which Romeo throws pebbles at Juliet's window to attract her attention. And "Cause you were Romeo, I was a scarlet letter" quotes Nathaniel Hawthorne's work "The Scarlet Letter". The heroine wears a red A-plate on her chest due to the crime of adultery. This line suggests that the young couple fell in love despite their family backgrounds, thereby making Juliet a symbol of family shame.

The second song is "Happiness,” which describes how the narrator leaves a toxic relationship. One lyric, "I hope she'll be a beautiful fool who takes my spot next to you," is a reference to F. Scott Fitzgerald's novel, “The Great Gatsby.” In the book, the character Daisy expresses her hope for her newborn daughter: "I hope she'll be a fool—that's the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool." This reflects Daisy's insight into the turbulent 1920s. She doesn't want her daughter to be smart and sensitive, but rather a "beautiful fool" who only needs to display her beauty and indulge in vanity, so she won't have clear insight into the world and will therefore suffer. Swift transforms this hope into the narrator's expectation for her ex's next girlfriend, hoping she will be a naive and beautiful fool, completely unaware of the "good man's" capacity to hurt people. But the next line immediately follows, "No, I didn't mean that / Sorry, I can't see facts through all of my fury," indicating that this emotion stems from anger and heartbreak, not genuine malice. These lyrics accurately describe the contradictory and struggling state many people experience after ending a relationship.

Besides the two songs mentioned above, there are also tracks like "Long Story Short," which references Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, and "The Fate of Ophelia," which references Hamlet. If you're interested in both Western pop music and literature, why not discover more literary Easter eggs in these songs the next time you listen to Taylor Swift?
